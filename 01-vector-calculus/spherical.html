3  Geometry and calculus on the sphere

3.1 Coordinates, metric factors, and area

Result and motivation. Spherical coordinates have unequal physical scale factors. The area measure becomes uniform in sine of latitude, which explains Gaussian latitude weights. Sources: (Arfken and Weber 2005, secs. 2.1–2.2, 2.5).

Let \(r>0\) be radius, \(\theta\in[0,\pi]\) colatitude measured from the north pole, and \(\lambda\in[0,2\pi)\) east longitude. Latitude is \(\varphi=\pi/2-\theta\). Write

\[ \mathbf x=r(\sin\theta\cos\lambda,\sin\theta\sin\lambda,\cos\theta), \qquad \mu=\cos\theta=\sin\varphi. \tag{M3.1} \]

The unit vectors \(\mathbf e_r,\mathbf e_\theta,\mathbf e_\lambda\) point toward increasing coordinates. Differentiating (M3.1) gives mutually perpendicular vectors of lengths \(1,r,r\sin\theta\). These are scale factors \(h_i=|\partial_{q_i}\mathbf x|\). The metric is the rule for squared distance:

\[ ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\lambda^2, \qquad dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\lambda. \tag{M3.2} \]

On the radius-\(R\) sphere \(S_R^2=\{\mathbf x:|\mathbf x|=R\}\), use solid angle \(d\Omega=dA/R^2\). Then

\[ dA=R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\lambda =R^2\cos\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\lambda, \qquad \int_{S_R^2}q\,dA=R^2\int_{-1}^1\int_0^{2\pi}q(\mu,\lambda)\,d\lambda\,d\mu. \tag{M3.3} \]

The last equality reverses the \(\mu\) bounds to absorb \(d\mu=-\sin\theta\,d\theta\). It uses a positive area measure, not an oriented two-form. The total area is \(4\pi R^2\). For \(q=\mu^2\), its global area mean is \(\frac12\int_{-1}^1\mu^2\,d\mu=1/3\); equal weights on equally spaced latitudes would give a different average.

3.2 Tangent differential operators

Result and motivation. Physical derivatives divide coordinate derivatives by arc lengths. These scale factors determine the spherical Laplacian and the correct signs of wind and vorticity. Sources: (Arfken and Weber 2005, secs. 2.2, 2.5).

The surface gradient \(\nabla_s q\) is tangent to the sphere and satisfies \(dq=\nabla_s q\cdot d\mathbf x\) for tangent displacements. For \(\mathbf F=F_\theta\mathbf e_\theta+F_\lambda\mathbf e_\lambda\), where \(F_\theta,F_\lambda\) are physical components, one obtains

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_s q&=\frac{\mathbf e_\theta}{R}\partial_\theta q +\frac{\mathbf e_\lambda}{R\sin\theta}\partial_\lambda q,\\ \nabla_s\cdot\mathbf F&=\frac{1}{R\sin\theta} \left[\partial_\theta(\sin\theta F_\theta)+\partial_\lambda F_\lambda\right],\\ (\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf e_r&=\frac{1}{R\sin\theta} \left[\partial_\theta(\sin\theta F_\lambda)-\partial_\lambda F_\theta\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{M3.4} \]

For the divergence formula, consider a small patch with sides \(R\,d\theta\) and \(R\sin\theta\,d\lambda\). The difference of meridional fluxes through its latitude edges is \(R\partial_\theta(\sin\theta F_\theta)d\theta d\lambda\); the longitude edges contribute \(R\partial_\lambda F_\lambda d\theta d\lambda\). Divide by patch area. Applying Stokes’ theorem to the same patch yields the curl formula, with counterclockwise orientation viewed from outside.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator is the surface divergence of the surface gradient:

\[ \Delta_s q:=\nabla_s\cdot\nabla_s q =\frac1{R^2}\left[\frac1{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\partial_\theta q) +\frac1{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\lambda^2q\right]. \tag{M3.5} \]

Define the dimensionless angular operator \(\Delta_\Omega=R^2\Delta_s\). In latitude and in \(\mu\),

\[ \Delta_s q=\frac1{R^2\cos\varphi}\partial_\varphi(\cos\varphi\partial_\varphi q) +\frac1{R^2\cos^2\varphi}\partial_\lambda^2q =\frac1{R^2}\left[\partial_\mu((1-\mu^2)\partial_\mu q) +\frac{\partial_\lambda^2q}{1-\mu^2}\right]. \tag{M3.6} \]

In three-dimensional space, the full scalar Laplacian is \(\Delta q=r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2\partial_rq)+r^{-2}\Delta_\Omega q\). Restricting to a surface and assuming radial independence are distinct operations; (M3.5) is intrinsic to the surface. Its units are field units divided by \(\mathrm m^2\).

3.3 Regularity, streamfunction, and the closed surface

Result and motivation. Poles are coordinate singularities, not physical edges. A smooth nondivergent tangent velocity on the sphere has a streamfunction whose mean is arbitrary. Sources: (Arfken and Weber 2005, secs. 1.16, 2.5, 12.11); fluid application: (Vallis 2006, secs. 2.2, 4.5).

All longitudes at a pole describe one point. A smooth scalar has a unique value there; nonzero longitude Fourier orders must vanish at the poles with the behavior derived in spherical harmonics. The local tangent basis itself depends on longitude, so smooth vector components need not be longitude-independent at a pole. Smoothness is a geometric condition, not a command to set all derivatives to zero.

For a smooth real streamfunction \(\psi\) with units \(\mathrm m^2\,\mathrm s^{-1}\), set \(\mathbf u=\mathbf e_r\times\nabla_s\psi\). Let \(u\) be eastward speed and \(v\) northward speed, so \(F_\lambda=u\) and \(F_\theta=-v\). Equation (M3.4) gives

\[ u=-\frac1R\partial_\varphi\psi, \qquad v=\frac1{R\cos\varphi}\partial_\lambda\psi, \qquad \nabla_s\cdot\mathbf u=0, \qquad \zeta:=(\nabla\times\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf e_r=\Delta_s\psi. \tag{M3.7} \]

To check divergence, substitute the first two expressions into \([\partial_\lambda u+\partial_\varphi(v\cos\varphi)]/(R\cos\varphi)\); the mixed partials cancel. Substituting into the radial curl yields (M3.6). Adding a spatial constant to \(\psi\) changes neither speed nor vorticity. The converse for every smooth divergence-free tangent field on the whole sphere is justified by the potential decomposition in the tangent-field decomposition. Until then, (M3.7) defines the velocity class considered. Domains with holes can require additional circulation modes.

Surface integration by parts has no external boundary term:

\[ \int_{S_R^2}f\Delta_sg\,dA=-\int_{S_R^2}\nabla_sf\cdot\nabla_sg\,dA, \qquad \int_{S_R^2}\Delta_sg\,dA=0. \tag{M3.8} \]

Here \(f,g\) are smooth real scalars. The second identity uses \(f=1\). Thus \(\Delta_sg=h\) requires zero area integral of \(h\). The coordinate cuts at longitude \(0\) and the poles are not physical boundaries; periodicity and regularity cancel their apparent terms.

3.4 Chapter summary and self-test

Main results and terms. Colatitude \(\theta\), latitude \(\varphi\), and longitude \(\lambda\) follow (M3.1); \(\mu=\sin\varphi\). Scale factors define distance, area, and differential operators. Area is uniform in \((\mu,\lambda)\), not \((\varphi,\lambda)\). The physical Laplace-Beltrami operator is \(\Delta_s=R^{-2}\Delta_\Omega\). With the convention \(\mathbf u=\mathbf e_r\times\nabla_s\psi\), vorticity is \(\zeta=\Delta_s\psi\). The Laplacian has zero integral and a constant nullspace.

Self-test. Verify \(\Delta_s\mu=-2\mu/R^2\). If \(\psi=A\sin\varphi\), find \(u\), \(v\), and \(\zeta\), including their units.
