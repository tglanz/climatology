20  Integral operators, symmetries, and learned maps

20.1 Kernels and spectral multipliers

Result and motivation. Many solution operators are global integral maps. Symmetry can reduce a two-position kernel to one multiplier per Fourier mode or spherical degree. Sources: (Arfken and Weber 2005, secs. 10.5, 12.8, 15.5, 16.1–16.4).

For input function \(f\) on a domain \(D\) with measure \(d\nu\), an integral operator has the form

\[ (\mathcal Kf)(x)=\int_DK(x,y)f(y)d\nu(y). \tag{M12.1} \]

The kernel \(K(x,y)\) weights the influence of source point \(y\) on output point \(x\). This operator is linear when \(K\) is fixed; a kernel depending on \(f\) can produce a nonlinear overall map. Its physical units must combine those of \(f\) and \(d\nu\) to give the output units. The Green’s operator (M5.7) is one example.

On a periodic interval, a translation-invariant kernel \(K(x,y)=k(x-y)\) gives circular convolution. With normalized integration \(dy/d\), its Fourier coefficients multiply: \((\mathcal Kf)_n=k_nf_n\). With unnormalized \(dy\), the multiplier is \(dk_n\). Normalization of the integral and of the coefficients must be specified together.

On the unit sphere, assume \(k\in L^2([-1,1])\) and \(f\in L^2(S^2,d\Omega)\), and interpret the expansions in \(L^2\). Let \(K(\mathbf n,\mathbf n')=k(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n')\) be isotropic, meaning dependent only on angular separation. Expand \(k(t)=\sum_{\ell\ge0}a_\ell P_\ell(t)\), with \(a_\ell=(2\ell+1)\int_{-1}^1k(t)P_\ell(t)dt/2\). Apply (M8.9) in (M12.1), using \(d\nu=d\Omega'\), and integrate against \(f\). Orthogonality yields

\[ (\mathcal Kf)_\ell^m=\kappa_\ell f_\ell^m, \qquad \kappa_\ell=\frac{4\pi a_\ell}{2\ell+1} =2\pi\int_{-1}^1k(t)P_\ell(t)dt. \tag{M12.2} \]

The same \(\kappa_\ell\) acts for every \(m\). For \(k=1\), only \(\kappa_0=4\pi\) survives and \(\mathcal Kf=\int f\,d\Omega\) is constant, verifying the normalization. An integral under physical area \(dA'\) supplies an additional \(R^2\). An arbitrary position-dependent kernel couples harmonic modes; choosing spherical harmonics alone does not make an operator diagonal.

20.2 Rotations and equivariance

Result and motivation. Equivariance means that transforming the input and then applying a map agrees with transforming its output. It is a precise property with specific group, field-type, and discretization assumptions. Sources: (Arfken and Weber 2005, secs. 1.2, 4.1–4.3, 12.8); operator formulation: (Bonev et al. 2023, secs. 3.2–4.2).

A group is a set of transformations closed under composition, with an identity, an inverse for every transformation, and associative composition. The rotation group \(SO(3)\) consists of real \(3\times3\) matrices \(Q\) satisfying \(Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I\) and \(\det Q=1\). On scalar fields define the rotation action \((T_Qf)(\mathbf n)=f(Q^{-1}\mathbf n)\). A scalar-to-scalar map \(\mathcal G\) is rotation-equivariant if

\[ \mathcal G(T_Qf)=T_Q\mathcal G(f)\quad\text{for every }Q\in SO(3). \tag{M12.3} \]

It is invariant if \(\mathcal G(T_Qf)=\mathcal G(f)\), as for an area integral. A vector field uses the action \(T_Q\mathbf v(\mathbf n)=Q\mathbf v(Q^{-1}\mathbf n)\) instead; rotating only scalar component images misses the vector rotation.

Rotations mix orders within a fixed degree. Consequently, a scalar linear multiplier shared by every \(m\) at degree \(\ell\) commutes with rotations; a separate arbitrary multiplier for each \(m\) generally does not. An isotropic kernel satisfies this property by (M12.2). Multiple scalar channels can mix through one channel matrix per degree, still shared across orders.

These statements concern full rotations of scalar fields with no preferred direction. A rotating planet has a specified rotation axis: holding that axis fixed reduces the physical symmetry. Geographic positional features, direction-dependent forcing, or latitude-dependent coefficients can deliberately reduce it. Continuous equivariance also does not guarantee exact discrete equivariance after approximate quadrature and unresolved nonlinear products.

20.3 Neural operators and loss functions

Result and motivation. A neural operator approximates a map between functions from sampled input-output pairs. Its spectral layer uses the same analysis-multiply-synthesis structure as a classical integral operator. Sources: (Li et al. 2021, secs. 2–4); spherical construction: (Bonev et al. 2023, secs. 3.4–4.2).

Let \(\mathcal G\) map an input field \(a\) (an initial condition, forcing, or coefficient) to an output field \(u\). A PDE solution map is an example; it can be nonlinear even though parts of its solver are linear. A parameter vector \(\vartheta\in\mathbb R^p\) defines an approximation \(\mathcal G_\vartheta\). For training pairs \((a_j,u_j)\), \(j=1,\ldots,N\), a quadratic empirical risk is

\[ \mathcal R_N(\vartheta)=\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N \|\mathcal G_\vartheta(a_j)-u_j\|_Y^2, \tag{M12.4} \]

where \(\|\cdot\|_Y\) is a specified output norm, including its spatial weights and units. Empirical means refer to this finite dataset; population risk is the expectation under a stated distribution. Good training fit alone proves neither small held-out risk nor correct behavior outside the training distribution.

A common hidden-field update, with layer index \(r\), is

\[ v_{r+1}(x)=\sigma\left(W_rv_r(x)+b_r(x) +\mathcal T^{-1}\!\left[R_r\,\mathcal T v_r\right](x)\right). \tag{M12.5} \]

Here \(v_r(x)\) is a vector of real scalar features (channels); \(W_r\) is a channel matrix applied at each point; \(b_r\) is a bias; \(\sigma\) is a nonlinear activation applied to each component, for example \(\max(0,z)\) for real \(z\); \(\mathcal T\) is Fourier or spherical harmonic analysis; \(R_r\) is a learned spectral channel matrix, possibly truncated by degree; and \(\mathcal T^{-1}\) synthesizes a field. The real formulation requires real \(W_r,b_r\) and conjugate-compatible spectral weights: Fourier matrices satisfy \(R_r(-n)=\overline{R_r(n)}\), with real weights at self-conjugate zero and Nyquist modes. A spherical matrix shared across all orders of a degree must be real to preserve \(f_\ell^{-m}=(-1)^m\overline{f_\ell^m}\). These conditions keep synthesis real before the real activation. A complex architecture instead needs an explicitly chosen complex activation. Input and output pointwise maps convert between physical variables and hidden features. The chain rule computes derivatives of (M12.4) with respect to \(\vartheta\); a gradient update \(\vartheta\leftarrow\vartheta-\eta\nabla_\vartheta\mathcal R_N\) uses positive step size \(\eta\). Learning these finite parameters does not mean optimizing directly over every possible function.

For a rotation-equivariant scalar spherical spectral layer, \(R_r\) must be shared across \(m\) at fixed \(\ell\), and the remaining operations must respect the same action. Spatial nonlinearities create higher degrees, so truncation and sampling again matter. A fixed-parameter continuum formulation can be evaluated at several resolutions, but accuracy transfer still depends on quadrature, resolved scales, data, and the complete architecture. It is not established by matching array dimensions.

20.4 An end-to-end mathematical example

Result and motivation. A single spherical diffusion mode connects geometry, eigenfunctions, PDE evolution, quadrature, statistics, and operator approximation without an implementation-specific assumption. Sources: (Arfken and Weber 2005, secs. 9.8, 12.6, 16.2); steps follow the preceding chapters.

Let the physical sphere have radius \(R\), diffusivity \(\kappa>0\), and real initial scalar \(u_0=A(\mu^2-1/3)\), where \(A\) is an amplitude with units \(U\). Set no forcing and solve \(u_t=\kappa\Delta_su\). Since \(\mu^2-1/3=(2/3)P_2(\mu)\), only \((\ell,m)=(2,0)\) is present. Equations (M5.1) and (M8.6) give

\[ u(\mu,t)=A(\mu^2-1/3)e^{-6\kappa t/R^2}, \qquad \langle u\rangle_A=0, \qquad \langle u^2\rangle_A=\frac{4A^2}{45}e^{-12\kappa t/R^2}. \tag{M12.6} \]

For the mean square, integrate \(\mu^4-2\mu^2/3+1/9\) under \(d\mu/2\) to get \(1/5-2/9+1/9=4/45\). A degree-\(2\) harmonic representation is exact. Three Gaussian latitudes integrate its squared amplitude (degree four) exactly, and five equally spaced longitudes suffice for general degree-\(2\) recovery. The decay time is \(R^2/(6\kappa)\); a forward-Euler update is stable for this isolated mode if \(\Delta t\le R^2/(3\kappa)\).

The time-\(t\) solution operator has multiplier \(e^{-\kappa\ell(\ell+1)t/R^2}\) at every degree. An isotropic learned linear map could approximate these multipliers. Changing a cutoff changes representable inputs; changing \(R\) changes physical decay; an incorrect area rule changes reported mean-square error. Agreement on one sampled field does not verify all these properties. If \(A\) is random with finite second moment, ensemble mean square replaces \(A^2\) by \(\mathbb EA^2\), which is a different average from the spatial one.

20.5 Chapter summary and self-test

Main results and terms. Integral kernels encode nonlocal linear maps. Translation invariance and spherical isotropy give spectral multipliers with normalization fixed by the integration measure. Equivariance commutes with a specified transformation action; invariance removes the transformed dependence. A learned operator approximates a function-to-function map using finite parameters and a stated loss. Spectral layers reuse classical analysis and synthesis, while nonlinearities couple modes. Equation (M12.6) supplies a complete analytic check of this chain.

Self-test. For the spherical kernel \(k(t)=t\), which degrees survive, and what is their multiplier? Does allowing a different coefficient for each \(m\) preserve all rotations? What must be specified before calling a model’s error “small”?
